# Anxiety & Depression — General Overview

**Source basis:** National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) public health publications on depression, generalized anxiety disorder, and related anxiety disorders. This is a paraphrased internal summary for knowledge-base purposes only — see "Important Notes" below.

## Overview

Anxiety disorders and depression are among the most common mental health conditions, frequently co-occurring with each other and with other conditions. About a third of U.S. adults experience an anxiety disorder at some point in their life.

## Depression — Key Points

Symptoms can include persistent sadness or low mood, loss of interest in previously enjoyable activities, changes in sleep or appetite, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and in some cases physical symptoms (headaches, digestive issues) without a clear physical cause. Not everyone experiences all symptoms — some have just a few, others many. The defining feature is that symptoms interfere with daily functioning and cause significant distress.

A specific form, perimenopausal depression, can affect some women during the menopause transition, with symptoms including intense irritability, anxiety, sadness, or loss of enjoyment.

If symptoms persist or don't go away, the recommended step is to talk to a health care provider. If someone notices these signs in another person, encouraging them to seek help from a mental health professional is the appropriate response.

## Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) — Key Points

GAD often co-occurs with depression, other anxiety disorders, PTSD, chronic pain conditions, cardiovascular issues, or substance use concerns — and the presence of multiple conditions together typically calls for a more comprehensive treatment plan.

**Treatment typically involves psychotherapy, medication, or both**, chosen based on individual needs, preferences, and clinical situation:

- **Psychotherapy options** include cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT). ACT differs from CBT by encouraging nonjudgmental acceptance of thoughts and feelings while focusing on engagement in meaningful activities, using mindfulness and goal-setting strategies.
- **Medication options** include SSRIs and SNRIs (antidepressants that also help anxiety symptoms), as well as anti-anxiety medications such as buspirone or, in some cases, benzodiazepines.

## Social Anxiety Disorder

Similar treatment principles apply — psychotherapy (CBT or ACT), medication (SSRIs/SNRIs), or a combination, individualized to the person's situation and any co-occurring conditions.

## Medication Safety Note (All Classes)

Children, teenagers, and young adults under 25 may experience increased suicidal thoughts or behavior when starting antidepressants or after a dose change, particularly in the first few weeks. Close monitoring during this period is recommended for people of all ages, but especially this group.

## Important Notes — Read Before Deploying

1. This is a general educational overview, not a diagnostic or treatment tool. The assistant should not attempt to diagnose a specific user with depression, GAD, or any other condition based on a conversation.
2. The assistant should not recommend specific medications, dosages, or whether someone should start or stop a psychiatric medication — that is a decision between the person and their prescriber.
3. **Crisis language requires a different response than general information.** If a user expresses thoughts of suicide or self-harm, the appropriate response is to share crisis resources directly (e.g., the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline — call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org — and 911 for immediate danger), not to discuss treatment guidelines.
4. Mental health information is especially sensitive to being presented as generic when a real person may be in distress — keep responses warm and point toward professional support rather than purely clinical.
