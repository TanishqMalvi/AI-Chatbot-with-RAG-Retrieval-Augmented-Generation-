# Hypertension (High Blood Pressure) — Clinical Reference Summary

**Source basis:** 2025 AHA/ACC/multi-society Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Management of High Blood Pressure in Adults (the first major update since 2017, published August 2025, still current through 2026). This is a paraphrased internal summary for knowledge-base purposes only — see "Important Notes" below.

## Overview

Hypertension is one of the leading drivers of cardiovascular disease, stroke, heart failure, and kidney disease. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline represents the first major revision in years and changes how risk is calculated, how aggressively borderline cases are treated, and how blood pressure itself is measured.

## Key Updates

**Treatment target unchanged, but applied more broadly**
The core treatment target remains below 130/80 mmHg for most adults — unchanged from the prior guideline. What changed is *who* qualifies for medication at that target. Patients with blood pressure in the 130–139/80–89 mmHg range (Stage 1) who are at lower estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk previously could often be managed with lifestyle changes alone. The updated guideline now recommends starting medication for this group if blood pressure remains elevated after three to six months of lifestyle intervention.

**New risk calculator (PREVENT)**
The guideline adopts the PREVENT risk equation in place of the older pooled cohort equations. PREVENT incorporates kidney function, statin use, and social drivers of health, generally producing a more individualized risk estimate — which is expected to bring more, often younger, patients into treatment-eligible categories than under prior guidance.

**Home and ambulatory monitoring elevated**
Out-of-office blood pressure measurement (home monitoring or 24-hour ambulatory monitoring) is now treated as the diagnostic gold standard, used to catch "white coat" effects and masked or nocturnal hypertension that a single office reading can miss.

**Expanded screening for secondary causes**
Screening for primary aldosteronism (a hormonal cause of high blood pressure) is now recommended for all patients with Stage 2 hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg) or treatment-resistant hypertension, not just selected cases — addressing a condition that's been historically underdiagnosed.

**Cognitive health link**
The updated guideline explicitly addresses the connection between high blood pressure and long-term cognitive decline/dementia risk, reinforcing blood pressure control as a brain-health issue, not just a cardiovascular one.

**Resistant hypertension**
For the first time, the guideline addresses renal denervation (a procedural option) as part of management for resistant hypertension that doesn't respond adequately to standard combination drug therapy.

## General Care Framework

- **Diagnosis criteria:** Stage 1 = 130–139/80–89 mmHg; Stage 2 = ≥140/90 mmHg, confirmed ideally with out-of-office readings.
- **First-line approach:** Lifestyle modification (dietary changes such as reduced sodium intake, regular physical activity, weight management, limiting alcohol, smoking cessation) is foundational at every stage, and may be sufficient alone for lower-risk Stage 1 patients.
- **Medication:** When indicated, early combination therapy (rather than single-drug stepwise escalation) is increasingly favored to reach target faster, particularly for Stage 2 patients.
- **Special populations:** Individualized — often less aggressive — targets are recommended for older or frail adults to balance cardiovascular benefit against risks like symptomatic low blood pressure or kidney injury from over-treatment.
- **Comorbidities:** Patients with diabetes or chronic kidney disease are treated with the same ~130/80 mmHg target, with particular emphasis on early, sustained control given their elevated baseline cardiovascular and renal risk.

## Important Notes — Read Before Deploying

1. This is a paraphrased summary, not the full guideline. Actual treatment decisions (which medication class, dosing, combination strategy) depend on a patient's full clinical picture and must come from a licensed clinician.
2. Risk calculators like PREVENT require actual patient data inputs (age, labs, etc.) to produce a meaningful result — this assistant should not estimate personal cardiovascular risk from a casual conversation.
3. If a user describes symptoms suggesting a hypertensive emergency (e.g., severe headache, chest pain, vision changes, confusion alongside very high blood pressure), the appropriate response is to direct them to seek emergency care immediately — not to discuss guideline content.
4. Treat any ingested clinical content as needing an annual refresh cycle, consistent with how often these guidelines update.
