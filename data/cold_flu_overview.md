# Common Cold & Flu — Clinical Reference Summary

**Source basis:** CDC Common Cold and Influenza (Flu) guidance, 2025–2026 season. This is a paraphrased internal summary for knowledge-base purposes only — see "Important Notes" below.

## Overview

Colds and flu are both respiratory illnesses but differ in severity, onset, and appropriate response. Telling them apart helps guide whether home care is sufficient or medical evaluation is needed.

## Telling Cold and Flu Apart

- **Common cold:** Symptoms develop gradually over one to three days, mainly affecting the nose and throat — runny nose, sneezing, sore throat, mild cough. Usually resolves within 7 to 10 days without treatment.
- **Flu:** Onset is typically sudden — fever, body aches, and fatigue can hit within hours, often described as feeling fine one moment and "flattened" the next. This rapid onset is one of the clearest ways to distinguish flu from a cold.

## Current Season Notes (2025–2026)

This flu season has seen unusually high activity, with an influenza A subclade (within the H3N2 family) driving most cases. It spreads quickly and isn't a precise match for this season's vaccine — but vaccination is still recommended, since it continues to reduce severity even when the match isn't exact. The CDC recommends annual flu vaccination for everyone aged 6 months and older.

## Self-Care for Mild Cases

- Rest and stay well-hydrated.
- Over-the-counter symptom relief (decongestants, fever reducers, throat lozenges) can ease discomfort but doesn't shorten illness duration.
- Testing early matters if flu is suspected: home rapid tests can check for flu A/B and COVID-19. If positive and started within 48 hours of symptom onset, antiviral treatment (e.g., oseltamivir/Tamiflu) is most effective.

## When to Contact a Doctor

- Symptoms that worsen instead of improving after several days
- Persistent high fever
- Symptoms lasting longer than 10 days without improvement
- Underlying health conditions (asthma, diabetes, heart disease, pregnancy, immune suppression) — people in higher-risk groups should contact a provider promptly even with mild symptoms, since early antiviral treatment is most effective soon after symptoms start
- In older adults specifically: confusion or dizziness alongside flu-like symptoms warrants prompt evaluation

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Seek emergency care (call 911 or go to the ER) for:
- Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
- Persistent chest pain or pressure
- Bluish, purplish, gray, or pale discoloration of lips, tongue, fingertips, nails, or palms
- Severe or persistent dizziness, confusion, or inability to stay awake
- Seizures
- Inability to keep fluids down, or signs of dehydration
- Severe or worsening symptoms in a young child, including trouble breathing, bluish skin color, not waking up or interacting, or being so irritable they don't want to be held

If unsure whether symptoms warrant a doctor visit, contacting a provider's office or telehealth line for guidance is reasonable even for mild-to-moderate symptoms.
