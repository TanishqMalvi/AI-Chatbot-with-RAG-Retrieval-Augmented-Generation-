# Asthma Management — Clinical Reference Summary

**Source basis:** Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2026 Strategy Report, the annually updated global standard for asthma management and prevention. This is a paraphrased internal summary for knowledge-base purposes only — see "Important Notes" below.

## Overview

Asthma management follows a stepwise approach: treatment intensity is escalated or reduced based on how well-controlled a person's symptoms are. GINA's guidance applies across primary care, urgent care, and emergency settings, and is updated yearly as evidence evolves.

## Core Treatment Approach

GINA recommends all patients with asthma receive inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing therapy — treatment with a fast-acting reliever inhaler alone (without ICS) is no longer considered adequate, even for mild, infrequent symptoms.

**Two treatment tracks:**
- **Track 1 (preferred):** Uses combination ICS-formoterol as both the daily controller and the as-needed reliever at every step. This approach — called MART (Maintenance and Reliever Therapy) — simplifies the regimen to a single inhaler and has been shown to reduce the risk of severe exacerbations requiring oral steroids, compared to older approaches using a short-acting bronchodilator alone as reliever.
- **Track 2 (alternative):** Uses a separate short-acting reliever (SABA) alongside a daily ICS controller inhaler, for patients who prefer or need this structure.

**Symptom-based steps** range from Step 1 (symptoms 1–2 days per week or less) to Step 4 (daily symptoms, frequent night waking, or a recent exacerbation), with treatment intensity increasing accordingly. At higher steps, add-on options include long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs), leukotriene receptor antagonists, and biologic therapies for severe, treatment-resistant asthma.

## 2026 Updates Worth Noting

- New safety guidance: patients should be prompted to seek medical care if they need more than a specified number of reliever inhalations within 24 hours — a sign that asthma control is slipping and treatment needs adjustment.
- New flowcharts were added specifically for managing asthma exacerbations in primary care vs. urgent/emergency settings, across both adults and young children — reflecting that the right response differs significantly by care setting and age.
- Continued emphasis on minimizing oral corticosteroid (OCS) use through better-optimized inhaled therapy and biologics where appropriate, since repeated OCS courses carry their own long-term health risks.
- Updated recommendations on vaccination (flu, RSV, COVID-19) as part of comprehensive asthma management, since respiratory infections are a common trigger for exacerbations.

## Recognizing a Worsening Asthma Attack — Red Flags

- Needing a reliever inhaler more frequently than usual, or it not working as well as it normally does
- Increasing shortness of breath, especially at rest or when lying down
- Difficulty speaking in full sentences due to breathlessness
- Visible struggling to breathe (using neck or chest muscles, flaring nostrils)
- Bluish lips or fingertips
- Peak flow readings well below personal best, if the patient tracks this

Severe or worsening symptoms that don't improve with reliever medication, or any of the visible distress signs above, warrant emergency care.

## Important Notes — Read Before Deploying

1. This is a paraphrased summary, not the full GINA strategy report, which includes detailed dosing tables, pediatric-specific guidance, and full evidence grading.
2. The assistant should not recommend specific inhaler doses or tell someone to step their own treatment up or down — that's a decision for the prescribing clinician based on the individual's full history.
3. Overuse of short-acting reliever inhalers is itself a safety signal (linked to risks like lactic acidosis from overuse) and should be flagged as a reason to contact a provider, not treated as a routine question.
4. Any description of acute breathing difficulty, blue-tinged lips, or inability to speak should trigger an immediate redirect to emergency care.
